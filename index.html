Overview

Adoption does not end at placement

Adopted people continue to navigate questions of identity, attachment, family membership, and belonging well into adulthood. Developmental and relational experiences can reshape how they understand their adoption over time.

Adoption research has historically centered childhood placement, permanency, and adjustment within adoptive families, often privileging the perspectives of adoptive parents, agencies, and helping professionals over those of adopted persons themselves. Existing theory does not fully explain how adoption continues to shape individuals across the life course, or why adoptees experience and interpret its consequences so differently.

The Adoption Lifespan Activation Framework (ALAF) proposes that adoption influences psychosocial development across the lifespan, and that adoptee self-appraisal and meaning-making are central mechanisms through which adoption-related experiences acquire psychological significance.

Rather than positioning adoption as inherently traumatic or universally beneficial, ALAF treats adoption as a complex developmental experience shaped by early relational events, family environments, cultural and social contexts, adoption practices, and institutional structures.

Central claim: The meaning adoptees assign to their experiences influences how, when, and to what extent adoption shapes identity, attachment, belonging, and psychosocial functioning across the lifespan.

ALAF grew out of The Adoption Trauma Spectrum, which maps lifespan activation points where adoption-related experiences may surface and resurface. The Spectrum contributes the activation dimension; ALAF integrates activation with self-appraisal, meaning-making, contextual moderators, psychosocial processes, and outcomes. The Spectrum identifies when adoption-related experiences may become newly significant; ALAF proposes how their meaning and implications may differ from one adoptee to the next.

The Adoption Trauma Spectrum

An integral component of ALAF that maps developmental and relational activation points at which adoption-related trauma, loss, and earlier experiences may surface, resurface, or acquire renewed significance.

The Adoption Lifespan Activation Framework

The overarching conceptual framework that examines how activation interacts with self-appraisal, meaning-making, identity, attachment, belonging, contextual conditions, and psychosocial outcomes.

Activation is not equivalent to outcome. The framework does not presume universal trauma, a uniform developmental pathway, or a predetermined consequence for any adoptee.

Mechanism & processes

Self-appraisal and meaning-making

Adoptees actively interpret and make sense of adoption experiences through personal, relational, and societal lenses. Meaning-making is influenced by lived experience, family narratives, relationships, access to information, and changing developmental contexts.

It is the hinge on which the framework turns: the same adoption-related event can carry very different psychological weight depending on how and when it is understood. Meaning-making operates through three interconnected, dynamic psychosocial processes.

1. Identity development

  • Sense of self and self-concept
  • Genealogical and cultural identity
  • Integration of multiple identities
  • Continuity and coherence
  • Identity exploration and revision

2. Attachment representations

  • Internal working models of self and others
  • Trust and relational security
  • Abandonment and rejection sensitivity
  • Intimacy and dependence
  • Relational patterns

3. Belonging & family membership

  • Feelings of connection and acceptance
  • Place within biological, adoptive, and broader families
  • Social and community belonging
  • Experiences of otherness or exclusion
  • Membership and recognition
Lifespan development

Activation points across the life course

Adoption-related experiences may become newly significant at developmental and relational transitions, prompting adoptees to revisit earlier understandings of separation, family, identity, and belonging. Activation points are not universal stages or a fixed sequence.

01

Relinquishment & early separation

The originating disruption of an expected biological and relational connection. Pre-verbal experiences may be considered within this early period without assuming conscious autobiographical memory.

02

Adoption awareness

Coming to know and understand oneself as an adopted person, including evolving family narratives and questions.

03

Adolescence

Identity, belonging, autonomy, and origins move to the foreground.

04

Birthdays & age milestones

Recurring markers that can carry the anniversary or symbolic significance of separation and family history.

05

Intimate relationships

Trust, closeness, intimacy, and fear of abandonment may be reconsidered in new relational contexts.

06

Parenthood & fertility

Pregnancy, infertility, parenting, and genetic continuity may reopen earlier questions about origins and family.

07

Reunion

Search and contact bring hope, risk, and renegotiated family roles.

08

Second rejection or relational loss

A later rejection, disrupted contact, or perceived relational loss may re-enact or renew earlier experiences.

09

Late discovery

Learning of adoption status or significant adoption-related information beyond early childhood, sometimes in adulthood.

10

Aging & health

Medical history, genetic information, mortality, caregiving, and legacy may bring adoption back into view.

11

Death of a birth or adoptive parent

Grief may be ambiguous, complicated, or disenfranchised, and may intersect with family membership and unavailable relationships.

The Adoption Trauma Spectrum is the foundational component that maps these activation contexts. The activation taxonomy should be treated consistently across the Spectrum and ALAF sites; related dimensions such as pre-verbal experience may be described as subdimensions rather than counted inconsistently as separate points.

Context & outcomes

Moderating contexts and diverse trajectories

Contextual and intersectional conditions shape both the experiences available to adoptees and the meanings they construct. ALAF does not locate responsibility for psychosocial outcomes solely within individual adoptees. Adoption-related circumstances, social structures, and subjective appraisal interact to produce diverse developmental trajectories.

Demographic and intersectional factors

  • Race and ethnicity
  • Gender and gender identity
  • Sexual orientation
  • Socioeconomic status
  • Disability
  • Intersectional identities

Relational context

  • Adoptive and birth family relationships
  • Partner and intimate relationships
  • Parenting and family building
  • Peer and community connections

Life conditions

  • Education and career
  • Geographic location
  • Access to information and records
  • Social acceptance and discrimination
  • Health and healthcare access
  • Community and cultural belonging

Adoption-specific supports

  • Counseling and mental health care
  • Peer support and adoptee community
  • Agency-based services
  • Records access and information
  • Mutual aid and informal supports

Adaptive outcomes

  • Identity integration and self-acceptance
  • Secure attachment and sense of belonging
  • Emotional well-being
  • Resilience and post-traumatic growth
  • Adaptive coping and meaning-making

Challenged outcomes

  • Identity distress or confusion
  • Relational insecurity
  • Chronic, ambiguous, or disenfranchised grief
  • Emotional pain and psychological difficulties
  • Trauma-related symptoms
  • Otherness, exclusion, or difficulties with trust and intimacy

Outcomes are diverse and nonlinear: a person may experience integration in one domain while another is reactivated years later.

Theoretical base

Core theoretical foundations

ALAF is developed through integrative theoretical synthesis, drawing on attachment theory, identity development, trauma theory, ambiguous loss, and lifespan developmental perspectives.

Bowlby; Main

Attachment theory

Early relational experiences shape internal working models of self and others that carry into later relationships.

Erikson; Grotevant

Identity development

Identity is constructed through ongoing exploration and integration, including adoptive and genealogical identities.

Herman; van der Kolk

Trauma theory

Distressing or unresolved experiences may remain significant and be reactivated in later contexts.

Boss

Ambiguous loss

Absent or inaccessible biological family members may remain psychologically significant, producing loss without clear closure.

Lifespan developmental scholarship

Development across the life course

Development involves continuity, change, transitions, and reinterpretation over time.

Additional scholarship informing the Spectrum

Relinquishment trauma, disenfranchised grief, cultural-racial identity, and adoptee consciousness inform the Spectrum's specialized attention to trauma, loss, and adoption-related meaning.

These scholars and traditions inform the framework; their inclusion does not imply endorsement of ALAF. Full bibliographic references should accompany the scholarly manuscript and website citations.

Empirical grounding

Informed by adult adoptees

The development of ALAF is informed by Adoption Across the Lifespan, an ongoing international mixed-methods research program with adult adoptees.

662
Survey respondents
90+
In-depth interviews with adult adoptees

Preliminary survey findings suggest that adoptees' subjective appraisal of adoption explains meaningful variation in attachment and identity outcomes beyond selected demographic and adoption-related characteristics. These findings inform the framework's emphasis on self-appraisal while remaining distinct from formal theory validation.

What the manuscript sets out

  1. The framework's foundational constructs: early adoption-related experiences, adoption context, self-appraisal and meaning-making, the three psychosocial processes, and moderating contexts.
  2. Proposed explanatory pathways linking adoption-related circumstances, social structures, and subjective appraisal to diverse developmental trajectories.
  3. Testable propositions about how, when, and to what extent adoption-related experiences are activated and reinterpreted across the life course.
  4. Priorities for longitudinal, qualitative, and participatory research.

Contribution

ALAF contributes to adoptee-directed scholarship by positioning adoptees not merely as subjects of adoption research but as interpreters and producers of knowledge about adoption. By centering adult adoptee perspectives, the framework challenges dominant narratives that equate permanency with resolution, placement with belonging, or adoption with a singular developmental outcome.

Researcher positionality

I am a same-race, domestic, infant adoptee and clinical social worker. My lived experience informs the questions I ask and my commitment to centering adoptees as authoritative interpreters of their own experiences. I recognize that my experiences do not represent those of all adoptees, and I approach this work with an ongoing commitment to reflexivity, methodological rigor, and accountability to the adoptee communities whose perspectives inform the framework.

About the author

Ashley M. Toland, DSW, LCSW, is an Assistant Professor in the Department of Social Work at the University of West Florida and a licensed clinical social worker in private practice in Alabama. She brings twenty years of experience across clinical social work, public and higher education, community mental health, child welfare, and clinical supervision.

Dr. Toland's scholarship centers the lifespan experiences of adult adoptees. She developed The Adoption Trauma Spectrum and leads Adoption Across the Lifespan, an international, IRB-approved mixed-methods study of adult adoptees conducted by a research team that includes both adoptee and non-adoptee scholars. Her work also addresses adoption-informed clinical care, child welfare policy reform, and the history of racial disproportionality in the U.S. child welfare system.

Her peer-reviewed work appears in Adoption Quarterly, Reflections: Narratives of Professional Helping, Social Work Education, and the Journal of Social Work Education, with book chapters published by Oxford Academic and Cognella. She has presented at the Society for Social Work and Research, the Council on Social Work Education, the International Conference on Adoption Research, the EUSARF International Conference, and the World Conference on Remedies to Racial & Social Inequality.

A same-race, domestic, infant adoptee, Dr. Toland earned her Doctor of Social Work from Tulane University (2022) and her MSW from the University of Arkansas (2008).
