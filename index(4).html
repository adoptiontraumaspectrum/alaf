ALAF · An emerging, adoptee-centered framework

Adoption Lifespan Activation Framework

Toward a lifespan developmental theory of adoption-related psychosocial experiences

Ashley M. Toland, DSW, LCSW · Department of Social Work, University of West Florida

Overview

Adoption does not end at placement

Adoption research has historically centered childhood placement, permanency, and adjustment within adoptive families, often privileging the perspectives of adoptive parents, agencies, and helping professionals over those of adopted persons themselves.

Yet adopted people continue to navigate questions of identity, attachment, family membership, and belonging well into adulthood, encountering developmental and relational experiences that reshape how they understand their adoption. Existing theory does not fully explain how adoption continues to shape individuals across the life course, or why adoptees experience and interpret its consequences so differently.

The Adoption Lifespan Activation Framework (ALAF) proposes that adoption influences psychosocial development across the lifespan, and that adoptee self-appraisal and meaning-making are the central mechanisms through which adoption-related experiences acquire psychological significance. Rather than positioning adoption as inherently traumatic or universally beneficial, ALAF treats adoption as a complex developmental experience shaped by early relational events, family environments, cultural and social contexts, adoption practices, and institutional structures.

Central claim

The meaning adoptees assign to their experiences influences how, when, and to what extent adoption shapes identity, attachment, belonging, and psychosocial functioning across the lifespan.

This shifts the theoretical focus away from externally defined measures of adoption “success” or “failure” and toward adoptees’ own interpretations of their lives.

The conceptual model

ALAF links early adoption-related experiences and the adoption context to psychosocial processes and outcomes through adoptee self-appraisal and meaning-making. Moderating contexts shape every pathway, and a return path carries later life events back into the meaning-making process, so meaning is revisited and revised rather than settled once.

Mechanism & processes

Self-appraisal and meaning-making

Adoptees actively interpret and make sense of their adoption experiences through personal, relational, and societal lenses. Meaning-making is influenced by lived experience, family narratives, relationships, access to information, and changing developmental contexts. It is the hinge on which the framework turns: the same adoption-related event can carry very different psychological weight depending on how it is understood, and when.

Meaning-making operates through three interconnected, dynamic psychosocial processes.

Process 1

Identity development

  • Sense of self and self-concept
  • Genealogical and cultural identity
  • Integration of multiple identities
  • Continuity and coherence
  • Identity exploration and revision
Process 2

Attachment representations

  • Internal working models of self and others
  • Trust and relational security
  • Abandonment and rejection sensitivity
  • Intimacy and dependence
  • Relational patterns
Process 3

Belonging & family membership

  • Feelings of connection and acceptance
  • Place within biological, adoptive, and broader families
  • Social and community belonging
  • Experiences of otherness or exclusion
  • Membership, recognition, and identity within families and communities

Lifespan development

Activation points across the life course

ALAF conceptualizes adoption as a nonlinear developmental experience in which meaning is continually constructed and reconstructed rather than fixed in childhood. Adoption-related experiences may become newly significant at developmental and relational transitions, prompting adoptees to revisit earlier understandings of separation, family, identity, and belonging.

  1. Relinquishment & early separationThe originating disruption of an expected biological and relational connection.
  2. Adoption awarenessComing to know and understand oneself as an adopted person.
  3. AdolescenceIdentity, belonging, and origins move to the foreground.
  4. Birthdays & age milestonesRecurring markers that can carry the anniversary of separation.
  5. Intimate relationshipsTrust, closeness, and fear of abandonment are tested anew.
  6. Parenthood & fertilityPregnancy, infertility, and genetic continuity reopen earlier questions.
  7. ReunionSearch and contact bring hope, risk, and renegotiated family roles.
  8. Second rejection or relational lossA later rejection can re-enact the original loss.
  9. Late discoveryLearning of adoption status beyond early childhood, sometimes in adulthood.
  10. Aging & healthMedical history, mortality, and legacy bring adoption back into view.
  11. Death of a birth or adoptive parentGrief that is often ambiguous, complicated, or disenfranchised.

Context & outcomes

Moderating contexts and diverse trajectories

Contextual and intersectional conditions shape both the experiences available to adoptees and the meanings they construct. ALAF therefore does not locate responsibility for psychosocial outcomes solely within individual adoptees. Adoption-related circumstances, social structures, and subjective appraisal interact to produce diverse developmental trajectories.

Demographic factors

  • Race and ethnicity
  • Gender and gender identity
  • Sexual orientation
  • Socioeconomic status
  • Disability
  • Intersectional identities

Relational context

  • Adoptive family relationships
  • Birth family relationships
  • Partner and intimate relationships
  • Parenting and family building
  • Peer and community connections

Life conditions

  • Education and career
  • Geographic location
  • Access to information and records
  • Social acceptance and discrimination
  • Health and healthcare access
  • Community and cultural belonging

Adoption-specific supports

  • Counseling and mental health care
  • Peer support and adoptee community
  • Agency-based services
  • Records access and information
  • Mutual aid and informal supports

Adaptive outcomes

  • Identity integration
  • Self-acceptance
  • Secure attachment
  • Sense of belonging
  • Emotional well-being
  • Resilience and post-traumatic growth
  • Adaptive coping and meaning-making

Challenged outcomes

  • Identity distress or confusion
  • Relational insecurity
  • Chronic grief, ambiguous loss, or disenfranchised grief
  • Emotional pain and psychological difficulties
  • Trauma-related symptoms
  • Feelings of otherness or exclusion
  • Difficulties with trust, intimacy, or family membership

Outcomes are understood as diverse and non-linear: a person may integrate one domain while another is reactivated years later.

Theoretical base

Core theoretical foundations

ALAF is developed through integrative theoretical synthesis, drawing on attachment theory, identity development, trauma theory, ambiguous loss, and lifespan developmental perspectives.

Bowlby; Main

Attachment theory

Early relational experiences shape internal working models of self and others that carry into later relationships.

Erikson; Grotevant

Identity development

Identity is constructed through ongoing exploration and integration, including the integration of adoptive and genealogical identities.

Herman; van der Kolk

Trauma theory

Unresolved experiences may remain latent and be activated by later stressors and relational transitions.

Boss

Ambiguous loss

Absent biological family members remain psychologically significant, producing loss without clear closure.

Empirical grounding

Informed by adult adoptees

The development of ALAF is informed by Adoption Across the Lifespan, an ongoing international mixed-methods research program with adult adoptees.

662
survey respondents
90+
in-depth interviews with adult adoptees

Preliminary survey findings suggest that adoptees’ subjective appraisal of adoption explains meaningful variation in attachment and identity outcomes beyond selected demographic and adoption-related characteristics. These findings inform the framework’s emphasis on self-appraisal while remaining distinct from formal theory validation.

What the manuscript sets out

  1. The framework’s foundational constructs: early adoption-related experiences, adoption context, self-appraisal and meaning-making, the three psychosocial processes, and moderating contexts.
  2. Proposed explanatory pathways linking adoption-related circumstances, social structures, and subjective appraisal to diverse developmental trajectories.
  3. Testable propositions about how, when, and to what extent adoption-related experiences are activated and reinterpreted across the life course.
  4. Priorities for longitudinal, qualitative, and participatory research.

Contribution

ALAF contributes to adoptee-directed scholarship by positioning adoptees not merely as subjects of adoption research but as interpreters and producers of knowledge about adoption. By centering adult adoptee perspectives, the framework challenges dominant narratives that equate permanency with resolution, placement with belonging, or adoption with a singular developmental outcome.

Researcher positionality

I am a same-race, domestic, infant adoptee and clinical social worker. My lived experience informs the questions I ask and my commitment to centering adoptees as authoritative interpreters of their own experiences. I recognize that my experiences do not represent those of all adoptees, and I approach this work with an ongoing commitment to reflexivity, methodological rigor, and accountability to the adoptee communities whose perspectives inform the framework.

About the author

Ashley M. Toland, DSW, LCSW

Assistant Professor, Department of Social Work, University of West Florida

Ashley M. Toland, DSW, LCSW, is an Assistant Professor in the Department of Social Work at the University of West Florida and a licensed clinical social worker in private practice in Alabama. She brings twenty years of experience across clinical social work, public and higher education, community mental health, child welfare, and clinical supervision.

Dr. Toland’s scholarship centers the lifespan experiences of adult adoptees. She developed The Adoption Trauma Spectrum and leads Adoption Across the Lifespan, an international, IRB-approved mixed-methods study of adult adoptees conducted by a research team that includes both adoptee and non-adoptee scholars. Her work also addresses adoption-informed clinical care, child welfare policy reform, and the history of racial disproportionality in the U.S. child welfare system.

Her peer-reviewed work appears in Adoption Quarterly, Reflections: Narratives of Professional Helping, Social Work Education, and the Journal of Social Work Education, with book chapters published by Oxford Academic and Cognella. She has presented at the Society for Social Work and Research, the Council on Social Work Education, the International Conference on Adoption Research, the EUSARF International Conference, and the World Conference on Remedies to Racial & Social Inequality.

A same-race, domestic, infant adoptee, Dr. Toland earned her Doctor of Social Work from Tulane University (2022) and her MSW from the University of Arkansas (2008).
